# Before Africa — Research Index

**Felix Lwova · Don Feloh · @donfelohke**  
**Status:** RESEARCH PROGRAMME · 7 November 2026

## Central question

What did African peoples call themselves, how did they understand existence, how did they organise society, and what did they know before external institutions came to define the continent and its inhabitants?

## Working proposition

The histories of African peoples do not begin with the word “Africa.” The name's exact etymology is disputed; Roman use for a North African region is documented, and its later expansion to a continental designation took place over centuries. The research task is to recover plural histories of self-naming, philosophy, governance, technology, memory and cultural practice—not to replace one universal label with another.

## Research map

- **FELOH Corpus:** master dossier, terminology, evidence standards and source register.
- **WARENA:** knowledge systems, technologies, infrastructure and governance.
- **GENJE:** livity as a carefully situated interpretive lens; rhythm, movement, oral knowledge and embodied practice.
- **IKAI:** existence, personhood, relational ethics and reflective agency.
- **NAKEBUSEN:** naming, heritage, identity and responsible cultural representation.
- **Gallery:** provenance, permissions and contextual metadata for visual research assets.

## Interpretive vocabulary

- **ROME:** a lens for studying imperial institutions, classification, administration and power—not the origin of all African institutions.
- **BLACK / LIVITY:** a contemporary philosophical proposition concerning living agency, embodied knowledge and self-determination. Livity is historically associated with Rastafari and must not be projected indiscriminately onto ancient societies.
- **UNNAMED:** not reducible to an externally imposed collective label; not literally without names.
- **UNTAMED:** agency, adaptation, negotiation, refusal and self-definition; not isolation or absence of social order.

## Research discipline

Plurality, primary sources, African scholarship, linguistic precision, contextual attribution, triangulation and explicit uncertainty. No universal “Alkebulan” claim without primary evidence; no invented indigenous terminology; no conflation of distinct peoples or periods.
